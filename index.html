About the game

Badminton is played with a racket and a feathered shuttlecock across a net, in singles or doubles, on a court just over thirteen metres long. Points are won by landing the shuttle in the opponent's court or forcing an error — first to twenty-one, best of three games.

What sets it apart from other racket sports is the shuttle itself. It doesn't bounce, it decelerates sharply after being struck, and it can be hit harder relative to its weight than almost any object in sport — which is what makes the smash so devastating and the net game so delicate.

Why the game holds up

Speed

Reaction windows at the top level are measured in fractions of a second — among the shortest of any racket sport.

Precision

A shuttle that doesn't bounce rewards placement over power — a soft net shot can beat a hard smash.

Endurance

A single rally can involve dozens of direction changes — footwork carries as much of the game as the racket arm.

Core strokes

  • ClearA deep shot sent to the back of the opponent's court, used to reset a rally and buy time.
  • Drop shotPlayed softly just over the net, meant to fall close to it and draw the opponent forward.
  • SmashA steep, downward strike hit with full power — the primary attacking shot in the game.
  • Net shotA delicate tap played right at the net, relying on touch rather than force.
  • DriveA flat, fast exchange at mid-court height, common in doubles play.

A short history

1870s

British officers stationed in India adapt a children's game called battledore and shuttlecock into a net game at Badminton House, Gloucestershire — giving the sport its name.

1893

The Badminton Association of England is founded and publishes the first formal set of rules.

1934

The International Badminton Federation is established, later renamed the Badminton World Federation.

1992

Badminton is added to the Olympic programme at the Barcelona Games, cementing its place as a global sport.

Today

The game is most widely played and followed across Asia, with China, Indonesia, and India among its strongest competitive nations.

The essentials

Racket

Light carbon-fibre frames, usually under 100g, strung between 20–30 lbs of tension.

Shuttlecock

Sixteen goose feathers set into a cork base, or a synthetic equivalent for practice.

Net

Set at 1.55m at the posts and 1.524m at the centre, exactly at head height for most players.

Court shoes

Non-marking soles built for sharp lateral cuts, not the forward-only grip of running shoes.
